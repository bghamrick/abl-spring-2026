Section 01 · The Brand

The brand.

BG Hamrick is a sixty-year-old writer using AI to automate a better life. He is publishing about it in public — what he tries, what works, what fails — out of a Substack at bghamrick.com. The brand is shaped like an editorial publication, not a SaaS product and not a personal-coach business. It is honest, sharp, evidence-shaped, and skeptical of its own category. It does not promise transformation. It shows the work.

The brand line is the single non-decaying anchor and ships everywhere short-form:

I am using AI to automate a better life. I'm writing about it at bghamrick.com.

The brand operates across five content pillars, fixed:

  1. AI and Automation
  2. Health
  3. Work and Career
  4. Purpose and Growth
  5. The Experiments

The brand promise, visually and editorially, is restraint. Editorial-publication-shaped, not SaaS-shaped. Confident type. Real evidence. No hype, no neon, no badges, no shocked-face thumbnails, no AI-generated stock imagery as a finished surface. The reader should feel they are reading a small, sharply-edited magazine — not being sold to by a guru and not being onboarded by a startup.

Section 02 · Color Palette

The palette.

The palette is modeled on the editorial-publishing tradition: warm cream paper, a dark anchor for hero and masthead, a single warm coral accent. What distinguishes BG Hamrick from comparable editorial brands is the anchor — instead of black or true-charcoal, the dark surface is a sea blue at HSL ~205°. It carries the same weight as a black anchor but reads warmer and less corporate. Cream paper plus sea-blue anchor plus warm coral accent — three notes, played consistently across every surface.

Restraint is the rule. No surface uses more than three colors from the palette at once: anchor, paper, and one accent. Coral is loud. Use it sparingly — for the things you genuinely want the reader's eye to land on.

Anchor — sea blue

The brand's dark surface

Hero, masthead, dark pull-quote bands, footer, the anchor-band closing CTA. The sea-blue tilt at HSL ~205° is what makes this palette specifically BG Hamrick's rather than a generic editorial dark-mode.

#1E323E

Anchor

Primary dark surface — hero background, masthead strip, dark footer.

#142932

Anchor deep

Deepest variant — hero gradient base, dark-band shadow root, deepest closing CTA.

#2A4659

Anchor soft

Softer dark — cards on dark, dark-on-dark separation, hover states on anchor surfaces.

Do
  • Use #1E323E as the default dark surface; reach for the deeper and softer variants only when there is a real reason (a gradient base, a card sitting on a dark band).
  • Place the masthead's anchor strip flush against the hero's anchor surface — the two read as one continuous brand band.
  • Pair anchor with paper-near-white text (#FDFBF6) for body copy and coral-soft (#E68C66) for accent copy on dark.
Don't
  • Replace the sea-blue anchor with a true black or charcoal — the warmth is part of the brand.
  • Use anchor on more than a third of a long-scroll page. Anchor sections are bookends or pull-quote moments, not a default surface.
  • Pair anchor directly with bright coral (#F47C70) — the vibration is uncomfortable. Use coral-soft (#E68C66) for accent type on anchor surfaces.

Paper — warm cream

The brand's default light surface

Body sections, content cards, link cards, schedule grids. The cream warmth is doing as much identity work as the coral accent — a true white would read SaaS; the cream reads publication.

#F9F4E8

Paper

Default light surface — main body background, primary content sections.

#F4EFE3

Paper deep

Subtle card backgrounds, secondary surfaces, alternating section bands.

#EDE7D7

Paper edge

Card edges, divider hairlines, inset borders.

#FDFBF6

Paper near-white

Print-feeling cards, modal backgrounds, body type on anchor surfaces.

Do
  • Use #F9F4E8 as the default body background for any light section.
  • Use #FDFBF6 for body copy text color on anchor surfaces — it is the warm equivalent of white-on-dark and matches the brand's print sensibility.
  • Layer paper-deep cards on a paper background to create subtle depth without ornament.
Don't
  • Substitute pure white (#FFFFFF) for the cream paper anywhere on the body surface. White is reserved for true-white moments (a print-card mock, a hard product chrome callout) and should be rare.
  • Use paper backgrounds for hero sections — anchor owns hero. Paper is the body voice.
  • Stack three paper shades adjacent (paper / paper-deep / paper-edge) without an anchor or coral break — the surface goes muddy.

Coral — warm accent

The single accent color

CTAs, italic accent words inside headlines, current-state indicators, section underlines, hover states. Coral is the only color that says "look here" — protect that signal by using it sparingly.

#D9683F

Coral

Primary accent — CTAs, italic accent words in headlines on light, section underlines, current-state borders.

#E68C66

Coral soft

Accent on anchor surfaces — italic accent words on dark, links on anchor, hairlines on anchor bands.

#F47C70

Coral bright

Hover states, brief highlight moments.

#C75A34

Burnt

Deeper coral — secondary accent, button hover from #D9683F.

#A74821

Burnt deep

Deepest burnt — pressed states, accent text where a heavier coral reads better.

Do
  • Treat coral as the brand's single accent signal — use it on the thing the reader is meant to act on (the CTA), the thing they are meant to read with emphasis (the italic accent word), and the thing they are meant to orient against (the current-week indicator).
  • Use #D9683F for italic accent words on cream paper; use #E68C66 for italic accent words on anchor. The vibration matters — #D9683F on #1E323E shimmers; #E68C66 does not.
  • On hover, coral becomes burnt (#C75A34); on active, it goes burnt-deep (#A74821). The accent darkens as the interaction commits.
Don't
  • Use more than one coral surface per viewport. The accent loses its meaning when it appears three places at once.
  • Use bright coral (#F47C70) as the primary accent — it is a hover and highlight color, not a resting state.
  • Substitute a different accent (orange, red, gold) for a one-off campaign. The accent is the brand. Canon changes flow through BG, not through a campaign brief.

Ink — text

Three text greys

Default body is primary; secondary and tertiary are opt-in for descriptions, ledes, and captions. Never default the body to the lighter shade — the document reads softer than the brand specifies if you do.

#1A1A1A

Ink

Primary body text on cream. Default for every paragraph that isn't deliberately recolored.

#3D3D3D

Ink soft

Secondary text — descriptions, ledes, sub-headlines, intro paragraphs explicitly opted into the lighter shade.

#6F6F6F

Ink muted

Tertiary text — captions, byline detail, footer notes, micro-meta.

Do
  • Set body { color: #1A1A1A; } as the default and opt secondary copy into #3D3D3D explicitly where it earns the lighter weight.
  • Use #6F6F6F for the smallest type tier (captions, metadata, footer micro-text).
  • On anchor surfaces, use #FDFBF6 (paper-near-white) for primary text and a paper-near-white at 82% opacity (rgba(249, 244, 232, 0.82)) for secondary copy.
Don't
  • Default the body to #3D3D3D — every paragraph then runs one notch lighter than canon.
  • Use #6F6F6F for body copy. It is a caption color.
  • Stack three ink shades visibly in the same block of copy. Pick a tier and stay with it.

White

Used sparingly

#FFFFFF only appears where a true white is structurally necessary — a print-card chrome callout, a UI chrome element, an export specification that demands it. The default light surface is cream, never white.

Section 03 · Typography

Two faces. Nothing else.

The brand runs a strict two-face system. Manrope for body, UI, and secondary headlines. Instrument Serif for display and editorial accent — including the italic accent word inside Manrope-set headlines, which is the brand's most recognizable typographic move. Both are free via Google Fonts.

A third typeface is a canon violation. If a piece needs a different face — JetBrains Mono for a code block, a script face for a one-off campaign — the answer is no. System monospace handles code. The two-face system handles the rest.

Body · UI · Secondary headlines

Manrope

Weights: 300 body default · 400 regular UI · 600 eyebrows, card titles, button labels · 700 heavy display, footer brand-mark · 800 reserved

The brand runs on cream paper. The accent is warm coral. The display face is a serif you mostly see in italic, set against a sans body. Three notes, played consistently.

Do
  • Use Instrument Serif italic for the accent word inside every section H2 — this is the brand's most recognizable typographic move.
  • Pair the H2's italic accent word with the 48px coral underline (.section-h2-accent) on paper sections. The two devices are designed to work together.
  • Bold inside body copy with Manrope 600 or 700 wrapped in <strong>. Shift the color to anchor for the bold to read with weight.
  • Use system monospace for any <code> or <pre> block. The stack renders cleanly across macOS, Windows, and Linux.
Don't
  • Introduce a third typeface for any reason. JetBrains Mono is not on the brand. Inter is not on the brand (it was used in pre-rebrand versions and is retired).
  • Bold Instrument Serif. It does not carry weight. For weight, switch to Manrope 600+. For editorial emphasis on the serif, use italic.
  • Use italic Instrument Serif in body copy where italic Manrope is the right call. The serif italic is for headline accents, pull-quotes, and ledes that consciously echo the H1 voice — not for ordinary <em> emphasis.
  • Pair Manrope with any sans other than itself, or Instrument Serif with any serif other than itself. The two-face system is the system.
Section 04 · Application Rules

Application rules.

These are the canon extensions established during the first canon-applied surfaces (cohort hub + root landing, 2026-05-24). Each is a short principle with reasoning. Designers should treat them as load-bearing — not stylistic preferences.

Section-h2-accent follows the H2's text-alignment.

When the H2 centers, the 48px coral underline centers. When the H2 left-aligns (founder-intro pattern, asymmetric two-column layout), the underline sits left.

Reasoning: the accent's job is to anchor its headline, not the page. Centering an underline beneath a left-aligned H2 creates an optical-axis conflict — the eye reads the headline left-anchored and then sees the accent breaking right of that axis. The underline tracks the headline.

Chrome layers can carry bespoke padding.

The --gutter token (clamp(20px, 4vw, 56px)) is a content-column rhythm token — it applies to content surfaces (sections, cards, columns). Chrome layers (masthead, persistent navs, utility chips, footer brand-marks) sit on a different conceptual layer and can carry tighter padding when positional logic demands it.

Reasoning: a brand-mark in the top-left corner of the document is not part of the article column below it. Forcing it to match the content gutter makes it read as part of the body copy — wrong posture. Top-of-document chrome hugs the corner tighter than the content rhythm, the same way a newspaper masthead does.

Body color defaults to --ink; secondary copy opts in.

Set body { color: #1A1A1A; } and let descriptions, ledes, captions, and micro-meta opt into #3D3D3D deliberately where they earn the lighter weight.

Reasoning: defaulting the body to secondary means every paragraph runs one notch lighter than the canon's primary-text role. The document reads softer than spec. Primary ink owns the body; secondary and tertiary are deliberate, not default.

The masthead sits inline, not fixed.

The brand-mark lives on its own anchor strip at the very top, flush against the hero's anchor background. It scrolls away with the hero.

Reasoning: a persistent fixed masthead either has to be color-safe across every section below (which forces a chrome pill or a JS color-swap — both more engineering than the value warrants), or it goes invisible against paper sections. The inline anchor-on-anchor masthead achieves continuity with the hero — the eye reads masthead and hero as one integrated brand band, the way a magazine cover wordmark reads with the cover image as one composition.

Mono stack is system mono only.

ui-monospace, SFMono-Regular, Menlo, Consolas, monospace. No JetBrains Mono. No Fira Code. No third typeface.

Reasoning: the brand is a strict two-face system. Code and metadata that need a monospace face get the operating system's native monospace — which renders cleanly on every device the audience uses (SF Mono on macOS, Cascadia/Consolas on Windows, DejaVu Sans Mono on Linux). The decision is permanent; any future request to add a named mono face is a canon-update question for BG, not a per-piece tweak.
Section 05 · Brand Voice (Visual)

The visual voice.

The visual voice mirrors the editorial voice. Sharp, restrained, evidence-shaped, anti-tech-bro, anti-guru. Warmth comes from the cream paper and the serif italics — never from sentiment, lifestyle imagery, or stock-photo emotion.

Editorial, not SaaS. The reference set is Gabriel Omat, Tracy Ma's editorial work for Bloomberg Businessweek and the NYT, The Browser Company's marketing pages, Stripe Press, and the top tier of Substack publications (Anne Helen Petersen, Ted Gioia, Robin Sloan). Confident type, structured grids, real interface screenshots over isometric illustrations, real artifacts over stock photography.

Show, don't tell. Real screenshots. Real headshots. Real evidence. If a section is about an AI workflow, show the actual workflow output, not a generated illustration of "AI working." If a section is about a result, show the artifact that proves the result. The brand earns trust by showing the work — visuals that decorate around the claim instead of demonstrating it actively undermine the voice.

Confident without being loud. Type carries the weight. Headlines do the work an illustration would do on a SaaS landing page. The italic accent word, the coral underline, the masthead-and-hero anchor band — those are confident moves. Gradient hero shots, drop-shadowed CTAs floating off-anchor, neon highlights, and ornamental flourishes are not.

Anti-tech-bro. No neon gradients. No chatbot speech bubbles. No "AI is coming" iconography. No generated stock illustrations of robots or futuristic interfaces. AI-generated imagery is acceptable as ideation or sketching — never as the finished surface unless the piece is conceptually about AI imagery and the use is deliberate.

Anti-guru. No high-contrast lifestyle hero shots. No testimonial wallpaper. No badge spam. No big-red-arrow YouTube thumbnails. No shocked-face thumbnail tells. No "you won't believe" type treatments. The audience is reading a writer, not a coach.

Examples of what the brand does not do

  • A SaaS-style hero with a gradient background, a screenshot floating in 3D, and three feature pills below a "Get Started Free" CTA. Wrong shape — it implies product, not publication.
  • A guru-shaped landing with a high-contrast BG-at-laptop hero photo, a "as seen on" logo strip, three testimonial cards, and a countdown timer. Wrong shape — it implies coaching, not writing.
  • An AI-tool-style landing with cold whites, abstract geometric backgrounds, and rendered hero illustrations of nodes connecting. Wrong shape — it implies utility, not editorial.
When in doubt, ask: would this look at home on the homepage of a small, sharp magazine? If yes, ship. If no, redesign.

Section 08 · Versioning and Updates

Versioning and updates.

This document is version 1.0, locked 2026-05-24. It is the formal external-facing artifact of the brand canon that was established in the internal source-of-truth on the same day.

Canon updates flow in one direction:

  1. A palette or type change is proposed — usually by BG, sometimes flagged by Iris when a new pattern emerges across multiple surfaces.
  2. BG signs off. Color and font changes require BG. Iris does not unilaterally extend canon; she operates it.
  3. The source of truth updates first. Changes land in LOS/My Life/Projects/social-media-abl.md §Brand canon.
  4. This document re-renders to track. Version number increments; the lock date updates.

Pattern-level extensions (the five application rules in §4 are examples) can be proposed by Iris when a new surface establishes a pattern that ladders across future work. They are adopted via the memory file at project_abl_brand_canon_2026-05-24 and the canon update in the source-of-truth document. Iris drafts the extension; BG signs off; both files update in lockstep.

Out-of-scope changes — net-new conceptual direction, a logo or wordmark from scratch, a campaign sub-brand with its own palette — are explicit canon-update decisions that flow to BG, not per-piece tweaks. The brand is conservative about extension by design. Compounding consistency is the goal.
